Dataset Shape

The encounter table is the operational core of the dataset and dominates the analytical surface area. Diagnosis and SDOH are both large enough to support meaningful subgroup work, while departments and TIGER act more like lookup tables.

  • encounters: 7,675,801 rows, 31 columns
  • patients: 947,685 rows, 12 columns
  • diagnosis: 1,531,262 rows, 5 columns
  • providers: 299,075 rows, 8 columns
  • departments: 11,597 rows, 9 columns
  • social_determinants: 3,977,901 rows, 5 columns
  • tigercensuscodes: 2,463 rows, 4 columns
  • Average encounters per observed patient is 21.34.
  • Only 38.0% of rows in the patient master table appear in encounters.
  • SDOH averages about 18.7 rows per patient among patients with any SDOH data.
  • The full in-memory footprint of the seven tables is about 7.6 GB.

Join Quality And Data Usability

Referential integrity is strong enough for cross-table analysis, but variable usability is uneven. The key analytic risk is not broken joins; it is heavy missingness or placeholder values in some demographic and administrative fields.

Join checks

  • encounters → patients: 95.8% match rate on PatientDurableKey
  • encounters → departments: 100.0% match rate on DepartmentKey
  • encounters → diagnosis: 100.0% match rate on PrimaryDiagnosisKey
  • encounters → providers: effectively 100.0% match rate on ProviderDurableKey
  • sdoh → patients: 99.6% match rate on PatientDurableKey

High-caution fields

  • SexAssignedAtBirth: 93.5% missing or unspecified
  • SexualOrientation: 95.0% missing or unspecified
  • CensusBlockGroupFipsCode: 64.5% missing or unspecified
  • SmokingStatus: 47.0% missing or unspecified
  • VisitTypeDescription: 15.8% unspecified

Practical takeaway: use encounter timing, diagnosis, department type, OMB race, and OMB ethnicity before leaning on heavily suppressed or administratively sparse fields.

What Stands Out

1. Utilization is highly skewed

The median patient has 8 encounters, but the mean is 21.3 and the maximum is 1,287. That spread is a strong signal that patient journeys are heterogeneous, with a mix of short touchpoints and a much smaller set of very high-utilization patients.

2. Fracture and injury diagnoses dominate

Top diagnosis groups are led by fracture categories such as lower leg and ankle, forearm, wrist and hand, femur, and intracranial injury. This suggests trauma-related journey segmentation is a natural first story.

3. SDOH is operationally valuable, not population-complete

The most common SDOH value is *Unspecified with 2,326,196 rows. Real responses cluster around transportation, food insecurity, utilities, financial strain, alcohol use, stress, exercise, and social connection, but coverage is selective and likely influenced by rollout and workflow.

4. Encounter workflows look mixed and broad

Common visit descriptions include in-person clinic visits, laboratory draws, physical therapy, imaging, acute visits, infusion, and obstetrics. This is a broad health-system dataset rather than a single service line.

Best Next Analysis Directions

  • Define patient journeys by PatientDurableKey, DiagnosisValue, and encounter date rather than by encounter type alone.
  • Focus on delayed follow-up and long-gap analysis after hospital or acute encounters.
  • Compare journey complexity across race, ethnicity, or cleaned SDOH subsets only after aggregating to patient level.
  • Filter placeholder diagnosis codes such as IMO0001 and IMO0002 before presenting clinical conclusions.
  • Use SDOH only after removing rows where DisplayName starts with *.
